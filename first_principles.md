# Nick Saraev: First Principles

Nick Saraev runs Leftclick (AI automation services) and Maker School, and narrates his own numbers on camera as he goes: [$500 to $21K per project](https://youtu.be/ite9u07MhsM?t=274), [$72,000/mo with one VA](https://youtu.be/eex1XkQwoMc?t=1819), [$300K/mo](https://youtu.be/hP8ecdTazBc?t=3531), [$400k/mo](https://youtu.be/UBsDnxNbR8Q?t=693), and [$500k](https://youtu.be/rlEiI6HiowU?t=183). The principles below survived only where the same rule showed up across independent lines of questioning and across at least two different videos.

---

## 1. Price on the value you deliver, never on the hours it takes

Hourly billing punishes the thing Nick is optimizing for (speed), so he treats it as structurally broken: "If you simply charge for current implementation time, not only are you shortchanging yourself, but you are also by default misaligning incentives" ([source](https://youtu.be/ite9u07MhsM?t=274)). He names the failure mode exactly: "Most people price their work completely wrong. They think: 'This project will take me 10 hours, so at $50/hour, I'll charge $500.' That's hourly pricing. And it's leaving massive money on the table" ([source](https://youtu.be/DbZotE0Ch0g?t=1928)). The replacement is value-based pricing, which he calls "my core principle for pricing... you price based off the value that your app delivers, not on your costs or your competitor's pricing" ([source](https://youtu.be/gcuR_-rzlDw?t=21824)). The mechanic is always value times a percentage, and the percentage moves with the deal: "the value or the price of your product is equal to the value you generate ... times like 10 to 20%" ([source](https://youtu.be/C05fWC2PpAE?t=586)), "The best-paid people in any organization earn approximately 20% of the value they provide" ([source](https://youtu.be/DbZotE0Ch0g?t=1928)), up to roughly 50% on a savings-based pitch versus roughly 20 to 30% on a revenue-based pitch ([source](https://youtu.be/ite9u07MhsM?t=600)), and "Once you have that number, charge somewhere around 10% of it" for a booked-meeting offer ([source](https://youtu.be/enGipMsZJaU?t=921)).

**Derived:** quantify the client's dollar value before naming any number. Pick the slice by deal type (about 10% per booked meeting, 10 to 20% typical, 20 to 30% on revenue impact, up to 50% on pure savings). Never quote a round number: "Don't charge $1,000 flat ever for anything" ([source](https://youtu.be/enGipMsZJaU?t=921)). State the 5x return out loud so the client sees the win-win.

## 2. Land with a fixed-price project, then convert it into a retainer

Nick never leads with the recurring offer: "the recommended thing that you do, any sort of agency, is you start with some sort of fixed project... they allow you to deliver a fast ROI... minimize friction because low cost... feel out a client... Then from there, what you want to do is you want to transition that into a recurring service" ([source](https://youtu.be/p374CjTavZg?t=186)). The same sequence is baked into his live offer stack: "you sell them on a fixed-price thing, something like $1k to $2k, framed around a specific result... If that lands ... pitch a monthly retainer" ([source](https://youtu.be/EwTgCOQ5OCw?t=795)). The reason to make the jump is margin arithmetic he runs on camera twice: a $1,000 one-time deal at $150 acquisition cost leaves $850 (85%), while the same relationship at $1,000/mo for six months leaves $5,850, "which he calculates as 97.5% margin" ([source](https://youtu.be/Z5rSDEbn7wU?t=455)); elsewhere he compares "a $54,000 LTV, versus 2,500 bucks with a $300 a month thing on a six-month... One's a 10x higher than the other. Can you sell that? If you can, obviously you should" ([source](https://youtu.be/A9NNHf4nOX0?t=993)). The retainer itself is never a bare resale of the build: "You don't just add in the service, you add extras to make the retainer a no-brainer" ([source](https://youtu.be/kChb6HrYOeE?t=239)).

**Derived:** open at $1k to $2k fixed, scoped to one named result. Pitch the retainer only after that project delivers. Price the retainer on LTV, not on the hours it will consume. Bundle extras (or fold maintenance into the base price, [source](https://youtu.be/rlEiI6HiowU?t=1287)) so the recurring line reads as obvious value.

## 3. Guarantee the outcome, but only the part you fully control

Guarantees are close to free in Nick's model because the downside is time, not money: "Guarantees just align your incentives with the customers, and then there's basically no downside. If you screw up, you're out nothing except for time, but you gain a ton of experience" ([source](https://youtu.be/4DEEO-XxN20?t=76)). He argues the guaranteed offer actually performs better: "you actually have a higher probability of success for offers you guarantee, as opposed to ones you don't. Why? Because you're aligning incentives" ([source](https://youtu.be/sYF3yfoiWtM?t=49)), and he refuses to soften it to protect the miss rate: "I would much rather give the money back the 5% of the time I miss than water down the offer" ([source](https://youtu.be/enGipMsZJaU?t=921)). The boundary is control: guarantee "meetings booked, not closed deals" ([source](https://youtu.be/Y1KVzKOsBEY?t=49)), because anything downstream of the client's own execution is not yours to promise ([source](https://youtu.be/vkB2PJm5bUQ?t=588)).

**Derived:** attach a guarantee to every offer you can. Write it against a metric you produce end to end (meetings booked, leads delivered, systems live). Never guarantee close rate, revenue, or anything the client's team executes. Budget for roughly a 5% payout rather than hedging the promise.

## 4. Never discount without cutting scope

A price cut on unchanged scope hands value away for free, so Nick treats the two as locked together: "Never power-game a lead for a discount, and never give a discount without cutting scope" ([source](https://youtu.be/rlEiI6HiowU?t=183)). The move he substitutes is structural, not numeric: "If somebody wants it cheaper, don't just give them a discount. Instead, remove line items from your scope or remove some functionality from the system you are pitching, so that you are not actually reducing the price or value of your service" ([source](https://youtu.be/bMmlCPLDk1c?t=917)). The other permitted lever is payment shape: restructure so the client pays more upfront in exchange for a proportionally better rate later ([source](https://youtu.be/rlEiI6HiowU?t=183)).

**Derived:** quote in line items so there is always something to remove. Answer every discount request with a scope question, not a number. Trade rate for upfront cash, never for nothing.

## 5. Sell the outcome, never the technology

"The biggest mistake I see AI automation agencies make is they obsess over the technology instead of the outcomes that technology delivers... most business owners don't actually care whether you're using GPT-4o, they don't care if you're using Claude, they don't care if you're using DeepSeek or any other custom LLM... What they care about are results" ([source](https://youtu.be/Fw8NGGnEchs?t=75)), a line he repeats near-verbatim a year apart ([source](https://youtu.be/L4Qbx8OM9l4?t=4909)). Applied to a specific deal he is even blunter: "The best way to do this would be to sell the deliverable, not the system that generates the deliverable. That's the most important thing" ([source](https://youtu.be/-fOhtHvitdk?t=690)). Same rule at the pitch level: sell the need (leads), not the implementation, and "Don't tell a prospect it's a Google Maps lead scraper unless they explicitly ask what it is" ([source](https://youtu.be/_wPHBUtGvfU?t=368)). He extends it to the label on the product itself, answering a member selling "AI employees": "in general, you do not want to sell an employee. Just like you do not want to sell a service. You want to sell the outcome of the employee or the service" ([source](https://youtu.be/A9NNHf4nOX0?t=761)).

**Derived:** write proposals in the client's units (meetings, hours returned, revenue), not in tool names. Keep the stack out of the pitch until asked. Name the deliverable, not the workflow that produces it.

## 6. Build what moves revenue, not what saves cost

Nick's stated ordering rule is proximity to money: "For services, always sell systems or things that are related to revenue, because they're much easier to justify and you typically have a higher perceived impact" ([source](https://youtu.be/MvuMuA8akX8?t=164)), and he claims the spread is measured: "Real data from 900+ breakdowns reveals the revenue proximity principle: front-end systems average 350% higher project value than back-end" ([source](https://youtu.be/T5-RXi-Zk_g?t=0)). Pricing follows the same gradient: "generally, the closer a system sits to revenue, the more you can charge" ([source](https://youtu.be/mWhbg_-HKzE?t=343)). The ceiling argument is why he refuses the savings pitch entirely: "You can only save 100% of the money a business already makes, but you can make infinitely more money. That's why I never sell AI cost savings, I sell growt[h]" ([source](https://youtu.be/enGipMsZJaU?t=170)), restated as "You can only save 100% of the money that you make, but you can make a business 10,000% of the money" ([source](https://youtu.be/vwgcMNuGKsU?t=99)).

**Derived:** rank candidate builds by distance to revenue (reach, acquire, convert, expand) and build the closest one first. Frame savings work, when you take it, as growth capacity. Expect back-office automation to price at a fraction of front-end work.

## 7. Keep a human on every touchpoint the client actually feels

Automation is gated, not default: "what is the magnitude of the outcome, and then what is the sensitivity to quality... If it's very sensitive to quality, as in if there are very small errors that create disproportionately large problems, I also step in. And if they're high on both, you absolutely want a human in the loop" ([source](https://youtu.be/MxyRjL7NG18?t=15606)). He applies it hardest at the front door: "be very, very careful if you are looking to automate the top of your funnel, because the first customer touch point with you is so important that, generally speaking, if you're going to put work anywhere in your business, humans belong at the front end... as technologies get better and better, the value a human will place on talking to another human versus a model is probably going to go up" ([source](https://youtu.be/nufugfMCnK4?t=837)). And on delivery: "I would not automate anything to do with human-facing work... I would always have a human being in the seat, because you're making way more money when it's white glove service than if you just automate it all" ([source](https://youtu.be/UBsDnxNbR8Q?t=693)). His own practice is more conservative than his content implies: "don't automate everything. Automate stuff, but don't automate everything... I actually automate a shockingly little of my work" ([source](https://youtu.be/2i2tPQKcQ4Y?t=2743)). The test he uses to decide is simply whether the customer feels the process ([source](https://youtu.be/yulWjh3rq28?t=21428)).

**Derived:** automate the zero-to-one grunt work behind the curtain; leave onboarding, kickoff, and updates human. Score each candidate on outcome magnitude times quality sensitivity before automating it. Control the human touchpoints on your own schedule instead of removing them: a batched five to ten minute client update every morning, with anything that arrives during the day answered at the next morning's window ([source](https://youtu.be/Zr4ypIb3KxE?t=266), [source](https://youtu.be/_Sb4lNGe_6M?t=673)).

## 8. Fix the process before you automate it, and only automate for a big win

"I don't think you should necessarily automate first. What I would do is process-optimize first. Don't look for things to automate" ([source](https://youtu.be/HWrly8ehW4c?t=200)); the sequence is build the process, then automate what already makes money ([source](https://youtu.be/HWrly8ehW4c?t=145)). Even once the process is real, there is a size bar: "I do have a rule, and my rule is the order of magnitude rule. I don't actually do this anymore unless I can get at least a 10 times improvement in a key metric, for instance, time, cost, or accuracy... unless the model expects that this is going to provide at least a 10x boost, I don't really recommend doing it" ([source](https://youtu.be/MxyRjL7NG18?t=12812)).

**Derived:** never automate a hypothetical or broken workflow. Prove it manually, then automate the part that already earns. Estimate the multiple before you build; below 10x on time, cost, or accuracy, leave it alone, because the implementation cost and added error surface eat the gain.

## 9. The simplest system that clears the bottleneck wins

"Here's the secret. The simpler the system, the more value it typically drives" ([source](https://youtu.be/BdCIfVfEvXw?t=167)), a lesson he had to unlearn himself: "When I was scaling left click to 72K a month, the first couple of months I had this infatuation with complexity too" ([source](https://youtu.be/BdCIfVfEvXw?t=190)), summarized later as unlearning that complexity correlates with revenue ([source](https://youtu.be/ll-HGTQ1vZM?t=6222)). He draws the upgrade lines mechanically rather than by ambition: an automation only becomes an app when "you need like a dynamic front end that isn't satisfied by the constraints of like a CRM, or maybe a Google Sheet, or maybe Looker Studio" ([source](https://youtu.be/C05fWC2PpAE?t=912)), and an automation only becomes an agent when the path is genuinely undefined: "Dynamic AI automation allows the AI to make decisions about which path to take... it might choose Path A or Path B depending on what it determines is the best approach" ([source](https://youtu.be/2nyU1v15gMc?t=1555)).

**Derived:** default to deterministic backend automation. Add a front end only when no existing surface (CRM, sheet, dashboard) can hold the interaction. Add agentic decision-making only when the path cannot be enumerated in advance. Judge a build by revenue produced, not by node count.

## 10. Template and productize before you scale

"I think that all automation services should be templated, because that's where you get the biggest leverage on your time in the industry... What templates allow you to do is collect that same thousand dollars, but then you only do maybe 20% of the work, maybe even less... you basically 5x your leverage" ([source](https://youtu.be/_mRlcEil5Dg?t=78)), which hardens into a selling constraint: "only sell projects you have templates for, because templating is where you leverage your time the most" ([source](https://youtu.be/_Sb4lNGe_6M?t=673)). Staying custom past the learning phase is one of his named worst mistakes: "Custom projects are fine when you're starting out, but I stayed in them way too long" ([source](https://youtu.be/eex1XkQwoMc?t=17)). The stage order he gives elsewhere is explicit: custom projects to pay down knowledge debt, then productize, then scale ([source](https://youtu.be/2i2tPQKcQ4Y?t=2368), [source](https://youtu.be/L4Qbx8OM9l4?t=3302)).

**Derived:** take custom work only to buy technical and market knowledge. Template the second time you build the same thing. Refuse offers you cannot template. Treat noticing that you keep rebuilding the wheel as the signal to stop selling custom.

## 11. Hire last, not first

"Hiring is sort of a band-aid solution to a deeper problem. If you're making less than $25,000 a month and feel you need to hire, odds are your business is suffering from a number of problems, but the main one, believe it or not, is not that you don't have enough manpower to fulfill, it's that the systems of your business aren't set up to decrease the amount of work it takes for you to fulfill" ([source](https://youtu.be/Zr4ypIb3KxE?t=105)), where he also argues technology now lets you hold off until $50,000 a month. The rule of thumb is a doubling: "push your bar for when you think you need to hire twice as high as you actually think, if you think you need to hire at $10,000 a month, don't hire until $20,000 a month, and instead focus on building systems that get you there without hiring. I made it to $72,000 a month with literally one VA" ([source](https://youtu.be/eex1XkQwoMc?t=1819)). The general form: "People tend to elevate before they exploit. So instead, you need to exploit before you elevate... Basically don't hire first, you hire last" ([source](https://youtu.be/miM6LCX08VQ?t=641)). And the cost is larger than the salary line suggests: three months of salary is a realistic all-in cost of a hire ([source](https://youtu.be/ZaE_v3UAk5I?t=2686)).

**Derived:** when you feel the urge to hire, systematize or template instead and re-check. Double whatever revenue threshold instinct proposes. Squeeze the existing system fully before adding headcount or tools. Do not hire inexperienced people to buy back time.

## 12. Manage by deliverable, never by hours

The anti-hourly logic Nick applies to clients he applies to staff: "As for how much time people work, all I care about are deliverables. I've tried tracking time; the only thing I actually care about is whether you do the deliverable... Do I care if they make $8,000 a month in 4 hours instead of 8? No, because they're still doing the thing I hired them to do" ([source](https://youtu.be/Zr4ypIb3KxE?t=448)). That is also why he prefers contractors: a contractor "is given a deliverable and a deadline and works according to their own process; you don't monitor or manage their hours," and he wants "people who are already great at what they do and have their own systems" ([source](https://youtu.be/cjwoKvlR0JU)). The metric he watches is headcount-normalized output: "I'm calling this a revenue per staff member just because this reflects I think a little bit better the modern hiring environment. We're not all hiring employees anymore. We hire contractors... this is important for me because I'm always trying to optimize this" ([source](https://youtu.be/r0c7RhMFcww?t=18975)).

**Derived:** write every role as a deliverable plus a deadline. Pay flat plus a trackable bonus, or a revenue share. Do not install time tracking. Track revenue per staff member as the ratio you are optimizing.

## 13. Test in parallel, cut the loser, then 10x the winner

Nick refuses to reason his way to the right answer when he can buy the answer cheaply: he deliberately ships copy he knows "isn't as good as it could be," runs roughly 500 to 1,000 sends per variant, then ranks by reply rate ([source](https://youtu.be/r9S8sFS7Yq0?t=484)). The loop is weekly and mechanical: "I'm always looking to get somewhere between 500 to maybe [1,000] emails sent in a campaign before I try and decide which one of the split tests or variants in the campaign is the highest performer... take the winner, you turn off the loser, and then you duplicate the winner and just change the copy up a little bit and proceed. You do that every single week" ([source](https://youtu.be/JPEMPQRNx1o?t=1510)). The same shape governs strategy choices: run three or four niches at once for 30 to 60 days, drop the two weakest, double down on the strongest, then set a 90-day no-change rule ([source](https://youtu.be/mE4gqfLBAYs?t=1481)), rather than trying to reason out the right niche in advance ([source](https://youtu.be/3-s_mcYO1aE?t=675)). The failure he flags most often is stopping at "it works": "they find a campaign that delivers some sort of result, books them some meetings, and then they think 'oh my god, this is working so great.' And then they just don't do more... My whole life is basically marked by me making similar sorts of mistakes where I just don't 10x it when I realistically probably should" ([source](https://youtu.be/u65TDfCJRAk?t=3777), repeated at [source](https://youtu.be/bTFCCeyvVOE?t=3276)).

**Derived:** never run one variant. Set a volume threshold (about 500 to 1,000 sends) before judging anything. Kill losers weekly and duplicate winners with one change. When a channel proves out, scale the input by 10x rather than leaving it running flat.

## 14. Act before you are certain

"You should be patient with results but impatient with actions... you should be extraordinarily impatient with action... The reality is the graveyard is filled with people that thought that they had more time" ([source](https://youtu.be/ZaE_v3UAk5I?t=1049)). The asymmetry he uses to justify it: "The cost of a decision is way, way more expensive than the cost of a slight mistake" ([source](https://youtu.be/dmANQbli0_I?t=1689)). The operational form is lowering your own bar: "you just need to non-stop spam action. When you non-stop spam action... you are going to have to decrease the threshold for perfection and certainty. So you have to be uncertain that what you're doing is going to work, and just do it anyway" ([source](https://youtu.be/BEUs0NHvn0g?t=991)), which is exactly why he sends copy he knows is not his best ([source](https://youtu.be/r9S8sFS7Yq0?t=484)).

**Derived:** ship the imperfect version today and let volume correct it. Treat indecision as the expensive outcome, not error. Reserve patience for results, never for starting.

## 15. Track the downstream KPI, and give the market time to answer

He tracks what causes revenue and ignores what merely looks like activity: no opens, only replies, positive replies, and booked calls ([source](https://youtu.be/T0fhHZZfFJI?t=224)), extended into the full funnel of "number sent, to replied, to booked, to shown, to closed" plotted over time, noisy day to day but trending up as skill improves ([source](https://youtu.be/tReerf2Hkh8?t=803)). The value is the attention the tracking forces: "What you don't track doesn't improve. What you track religiously tends to improve a little bit faster" ([source](https://youtu.be/Zr4ypIb3KxE?t=791)). And he explicitly warns against reacting to noise: "If you send 1,000 and then in a 24-hour time period try and make that decision, what you're basically doing is you're seeing noise... you basically have to continue to persist in the market regardless of whether or not the data says you should iterate, until you get enough data for those error bounds to kind of go down... markets take a lot longer to react to than you think" ([source](https://youtu.be/Wn09lLbdTkg?t=1340)). His retrospective window is long: judge the first stretch of any effort in hindsight, not while you are in it ([source](https://youtu.be/tReerf2Hkh8?t=968)).

**Derived:** report sent, replied, positively replied, booked, shown, closed. Delete open rate from the dashboard. Do not change a campaign inside 24 hours of data. Review performance on a months-long window, not a daily one.

## 16. Consistency is the whole game, and giving up is the only failure mode

"Successful automation entrepreneurs aren't special. They're consistent. Just repeat after me: humans are persistence hunters. This is really the one thing that makes us stand out... It's just the ability to dog after it for long enough... if you just continue doing it for long enough, you continue doing those boring, unglamorous, unsexy lead gen activities for 2 years, you'll eventually make it" ([source](https://youtu.be/52yOx4nAGM8?t=619)), delivered nearly verbatim again a year later ([source](https://youtu.be/L4Qbx8OM9l4?t=2580)). He builds it from the smallest possible increment rather than intensity: "The most important thing for me is just momentum, just making sure that no matter what, I always put in some small amount of daily effort... I never let myself go all or nothing. I'm never like 100% and then 0%" ([source](https://youtu.be/fxs89mKfAkE?t=52)), because "Motivation is fleeting... it's not something you can realistically, reliably depend on day to day" ([source](https://youtu.be/sEDIYVhuyOU?t=442)). He pays real money for it: "the quality of my content, or rather the degree of virality of any individual video, does not matter as much as just staying consistent. I would rather get content in, if it's 90% quality, if it lets me post 4 days a week, than try and aim for perfection with every video and paralyze myself in fear... That is at the cost of my 1 million subscriber goal, unfortunately" ([source](https://youtu.be/rlEiI6HiowU?t=1747)). The stance underneath: "I only have two possible outcomes, either I win or I give up. If you just don't give up, it is inevitable that you will win, because giving up is the only failure mode" ([source](https://youtu.be/G0nlN4HqM7w?t=1660)).

**Derived:** set the daily minimum low enough that you never miss it, then raise it gradually. Accept 90% quality to protect cadence. Treat poor strategy, poor tactics, and a bad emotional week as symptoms, not failures. Never go all or nothing.

## 17. Remove the option to be distracted, do not resist it

"The crux of it is not to never get distracted. The crux of it is to get 1% better at avoiding distraction every day. And then also to eliminate the possibility that distraction even occurs by eliminating it as an option... you will never ever again be annoyed, because it just will not happen, because it cannot happen" ([source](https://youtu.be/tWQLVmL8HyI?t=1159)). He frames the payoff as competitive, not personal: "focus and discipline are the ultimate moat in today's generation and beyond... It matters way less what you know. It matters how quickly you can learn. And the only way that you learn stuff quickly is with focus and discipline... The ability to say no to new opportunities is, I think, another really big and understated skill" ([source](https://youtu.be/dZt4Ye5wHHs?t=285)). The industry-specific version is his most repeated warning: AI automation has more shiny objects than any other industry, and chasing them is the biggest contributor to not signing clients ([source](https://youtu.be/mE4gqfLBAYs?t=1005)), which he treats with a future-ideas list plus a fixed commitment window ([source](https://youtu.be/mE4gqfLBAYs?t=1550)).

**Derived:** block the distraction at the system level rather than deciding against it each time. Keep a future-ideas list so a new tool gets written down instead of started. Run a 90-day no-change rule: no new business, no new vertical, no side projects.

## 18. Earn the win before you talk about it

"By choosing to document your journey, you are choosing to allocate a portion of the time that you would have spent to succeeding to just talking about succeeding... my recommendation to everybody is not to document your journey right now. It is to spend all of your available resources becoming a person of renown, and then documenting your journey afterwards" ([source](https://youtu.be/sD4qvSjMj4w?t=479)). The reason is blunt: "Nobody cares about your journey until you've already made it. And so if you spend any time documenting your journey before you've already made it, this time is almost completely wasted" ([source](https://youtu.be/w6WQetiebq0?t=970)). He gives a threshold and hedges it: "I highly recommend against documenting your journey publicly for the first, I don't know, 15 or $20,000 a month" ([source](https://youtu.be/xwZW4hzTVZQ?t=632)), and elsewhere "before you start making videos about a thing, especially when it comes to money, get to the point where you've achieved more than 99% of people at that thing" ([source](https://youtu.be/5goQTKl2cKI?t=326)). Until then the channel is outbound: "you should not make content. Actually, you should just send direct messages to people... You should spin up cold email campaigns targeting a bunch of different niches and then test... The whole content thing, not very good. It just takes too long" ([source](https://youtu.be/R7eSLwqPvWE?t=387)). The sequencing he prescribes to his own members: "What I would do first is knock something out of the park and get some social proof under your belt. Get a win in the arena of business. And then, once you have the win, you can start talking about it, and combine those two to make a wins machine, as I have" ([source](https://youtu.be/-fOhtHvitdk)).

**Derived:** acquire clients through direct outreach until roughly $10,000 to $20,000 a month. Do not start a channel to find your first customers. Start content when you have a result that beats 99% of the people watching, and make the result the content.

## What he refuses

- **To bill by the hour.** "If you position your experience in hours, you're almost always shortchanging yourself" ([source](https://youtu.be/ite9u07MhsM?t=274)).
- **To quote a round number.** "Don't charge $1,000 flat ever for anything. Don't ever charge $300 recurring ever for anything" ([source](https://youtu.be/ulhOFwhK-KY?t=877)).
- **To discount without removing scope.** "Never power-game a lead for a discount, and never give a discount without cutting scope" ([source](https://youtu.be/rlEiI6HiowU?t=183)).
